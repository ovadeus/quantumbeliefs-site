About the book

The part of a belief that surveys throw away is not noise. It is the most important part!

Every poll and survey asks you to take something alive, leaning two ways at once, snagged on a memory, still forming, and flatten it into a single mark. Everything that did not fit gets thrown away. The contemplation, the subtle weighing of thoughts, the change of mind, the quiet “it depends” disappears into that single mark. Classical research labels that inconsequential noise. It is not.

Quantum Beliefs is a clear, visual introduction to the hidden structure of how we actually hold beliefs. Drawing on quantum cognition, which applies mathematical tools from quantum theory to models of judgment and decision-making, designer and data visualizer David Edwin Meyers shows why a single answer is never the whole story.

Inside, you’ll meet three concepts for exploring the uncertainty between a question and a committed answer:

  • The Cognitive Qubit
  • The Collapse Gap
  • The Entropy Slider

Use them to understand your users and audiences with more depth than a rating scale allows, and to read whole populations as a living picture of how people lean, hesitate, and shift together. Designers, researchers, social scientists, and psychologists will find a shared language for the part of belief that no single answer can hold.

This book does not claim your brain is a quantum computer. It borrows the mathematics with care and pairs it with concrete pictures and everyday examples, so ordinary readers can finally grasp ideas that have stayed locked inside technical papers.

You were never a single answer. This book helps you meet the person behind the mark.

About the author

David Edwin Meyers

David Edwin Meyers is the author of Quantum Beliefs and creator of the Cognitive Qubit and the Belief Genome Project, and designer of its measurement instrument. David is a creative polymath, researcher, data visualizer, computer programmer, and author who has spent over four decades exploring the intersection of art, technology, user experience psychology, and human self-understanding.

After years of working across creative disciplines, a single realization crystallized: every problem we face as individuals and as a species ultimately traces back to beliefs we have never examined or recorded at a quantum level. The Belief Genome Project was born from the conviction that self-knowledge, made precise and measurable, is a missing variable in every equation we’ve tried to solve.

From the preface

A Note on Phase

When I was a boy, I discovered a picture of the atom in my science book. A small cluster of spheres sat in the middle, with other spheres circling it like tiny planets. I loved that picture. Then my teacher told me something that stopped me cold. Nobody had ever seen an atom. Nobody could. So I asked the obvious question. If no one has ever seen an atom, how did someone draw one? Where did the picture come from, and how do we know it’s real?

I don’t remember the answer I got. I remember the question because, as a data visualization designer, I have been asking a version of it ever since.

Here is the answer I wish I had been given, in words a boy of ten could carry home.

You’re right, nobody has ever seen one. But you can know something is there without seeing it. You can’t see the wind, but you can watch it bend the trees. Scientists watched what tiny things do, the way you watch the trees, and then they drew the best picture they could of what must be causing it. The picture isn’t a photograph. It’s a helper. It gives your mind something to hold so you can think about a thing too small to see.

David Edwin Meyers
